# The Unexpected AI Leaderboard

*This one's about the ads*

• • •

Everyone's ranking AI models right now. Benchmarks, context windows, coding accuracy. Reviews splitting hairs over PhD-level math when most are asking for dinner recommendations or making memes.

And here I am adding to the pile. Different take though. No benchmarks. At least not the typical ones. As AI goes mainstream, the commercials keep showing up. And they're trying to tell us something.

We're a big sports family. The NBA is number one in the house, so games are on often. NFL Sundays too, but mostly the evening games. After a while you start to notice the AI commercials. Then when you really pay attention, there are quite a few of them.

Most flashed by me at first. A few made me look up. One made me say something out loud to my wife. She does not care about AI commercials.

That difference is the whole story.

When I really started to think about it, these ads fall into three categories.

**Sitcom energy.** Low stakes, domestic chaos, everything resolved in 30 seconds. The laugh track is implied.

**Rocky energy.** Training montage vibes. You're gonna do the thing. The music swells.

**Fight Club energy.** Swagger, edge, something to prove.

And then some were actually missing. Which is its own kind of move.

Here's how I see it.

• • •

## Google Gemini: Sitcom Energy

It didn't really register until it did. And when it did, it felt nice. Like that dependable sitcom you've watched for the 30th time right before bed.

"Mr. Fuzzy's Big Adventure" is the flagship spot. Parents leave their kid's stuffed lamb on a plane. Meltdown incoming. So they use Gemini to generate a fake story about the lamb's little vacation while they wait for it to be returned. Cute videos of Mr. Fuzzy at the beach. Happy kid. Crisis averted.

It's warm. It's competent. It's network television. Cold open, minor problem, resolution before the first commercial break.

(I should mention: my kids are older now, but I remember this phase. The stuffed animal panic IS real. If this ad had existed when my daughter was four, it would have hit home more. So I'm not saying it doesn't work. I'm saying it didn't make me feel anything about Gemini specifically. It made me feel something about parenting.)

The other spots follow the same formula. A cheese shop needs product descriptions. A nervous parent preps for a job interview. Low stakes, domestic scenarios, the AI as helpful background utility.

**The read:** Google is talking to busy parents who just want one less thing to think about. The AI is a helpful appliance. A Roomba for your to-do list. Nothing wrong with that. It's just not what makes me lean forward.

• • •

## Meta AI: More Sitcom Energy, Running Faster

"Frank" is actually a well-made little short film. Dad discovers his daughter's goldfish is dead. Assumes the cat ate it. Panics. Uses Meta AI to identify the breed and find a store that carries it. Races across town. Gets home just in time. Twist: the fish wasn't dead. The kid took it to school for show and tell.

Good editing. Nice pacing. Classic sitcom structure: bumbling dad, ticking clock, misunderstanding, punchline. If this were an ad for insurance or beer, it would be doing exactly what it's supposed to do.

(Side note: The aquarium hobbyist community on Reddit had thoughts. The fish was shown in a tiny lidless bowl sitting right next to the cat. Someone commented: "You'd think an AI would speak up about this." Which is actually a pretty good point about where we are with these tools. They can identify a fish breed instantly. They can't tell you the fish might be in some danger.)

Their Ray-Ban spots go younger and faster. POV shots, mixed reality overlays, people jogging through the city with AI along for the ride. The message is clear: we're already in your apps, already on your face, might as well be useful.

Meta's ads have a point of view. It's "helpful." Not "let's go do something amazing." Just… helpful. They make AI feel grounded and normal. Present everywhere. Running alongside you. But not pushing you toward anything. Not making you feel like you could do something you couldn't do before.

**The read:** AI as a background utility. The electricity in the walls. It's there, it works, you don't think about it. For a lot of people, that's probably exactly what they want.

• • •

## Grok: No-Show Energy

To my knowledge, Grok didn't run any commercials. At least I haven't seen one.

No broadcast spots. No agency. No cinematic shorts. Elon mentioned on X Spaces that they'd be putting ads inside Grok's responses to help cover infrastructure costs. But that's different. Not their own ads. Just ad dollars running through the product.

X is its own distribution channel. And if you've used Grok even a little, you know the personality is the brand.

Features like "Roast Me" went viral on their own. Screenshots of Grok insulting people's profiles became the marketing. Which, if you think about it, is kind of brilliant. The product is weird and opinionated, and so is the marketing. Or lack of marketing. Same thing.

Here's what's interesting: Grok actually has genuine attitude. I've used it plenty. For certain things, I really like how it approaches a problem differently than the others. It's got a perspective. There's raw material here for a great campaign. Provocative, memorable, differentiated.

They chose not to use it.

The silence says something. Maybe it's principled. We don't do Madison Avenue games. Maybe it's practical. Maybe the product has had enough controversy without putting it in front of mainstream audiences during NBA games. Or worse, the NFL.

Either way, not showing up is a choice. And for the people who distrust advertising itself, it might be the right one.

**The read:** Anti-marketing as marketing. The court jester who won't perform on command.

• • •

Bob Dylan once wrote: "Advertising signs they con you into thinking you're the one that can do what's never been done, that can win what's never been won."

He's not wrong. Some of it is a con.

But, like, sometimes I want to be conned. Or really, I want to confirm what I've maybe already bought into. The sitcom ads don't do that. They just remind me that life is busy and help is available.

These are the ads that try to make you believe.

• • •

## ChatGPT: Rocky Energy

These ads hit that Dylan nerve. They made me pay attention.

"Pull-Up" shows a guy alone at an outdoor gym at night. Grinding through a workout routine he built with ChatGPT. The lighting is moody, there's real effort on his face, and Simple Minds is playing underneath. "Someone, Somewhere in Summertime." You know the song. Or actually, I'm not sure I do. But I know that '80s feeling instantly.

"Road Trip" follows two siblings driving toward the Blue Ridge Mountains at golden hour. "Dish" shows a woman cooking for a date, asking for a recipe that says "I like you but want to play it cool."

I liked these so much I wanted to know more. From what I found, OpenAI went with a smaller indie agency for this campaign. They shot on 35mm film with custom lenses. For a purely digital product, they chose analog warmth. Visible grain. The texture of actual film stock.

Smart choice. In a world full of AI-generated slop, shooting on film is a credibility signal. It says: we care about craft. We're not just here to automate everything.

The structure is smart too. You don't see the interface until the very end, scrolling up like credits after a scene. The human is the protagonist. The AI is the crew helping them get over the hump. Giving them a little confidence to try the thing.

But here's what didn't quite hit for me. Something about the music.

It's not triumphant. It's more… end of the movie and I'm not sure how I feel just yet. There's an '80s melancholy underneath everything. Almost like The Wonder Years (Gen X and older millennials, remember the opening credits?), if that makes sense. Where the voiceover is always a little sad, even when something good is happening. Like you're watching a memory instead of a moment.

I read that the agency wanted each spot to feel like "the final scene of a movie." And maybe that's the thing. Final scenes are often bittersweet. You got what you wanted, but it's over now. The credits are rolling.

So why don't I feel more confident at the end of these ads? I'm not sure. They're beautifully made. I respect the craft. They assume you're trying to do something that matters. But emotionally, it's a little unresolved. Rocky wins, but you tear up when he's walking out of the ring.

**The read:** Aspirational with a hint of something I can't quite name. These ads respect the viewer. I just wish I walked away feeling like I could conquer something instead of feeling like something is already ending.

• • •

## Claude: Fight Club Energy

This one is different.

We were watching an NBA game. Luka and the Lakers, early season. We watch intently but usually get up during commercials to do things around the house, answer texts, whatever. A commercial break started and I did what I always do, which is look down at my phone.

And then the music started.

I looked up. Phone down. I watched the entire thing.

MF DOOM. "All Caps."

If you know, you know.

DOOM is the rapper's rapper. Underground canon. Intricate rhyme schemes, supervillain persona, complete rejection of mainstream industry norms. He wore a metal mask because he wanted the music to matter more than his face. He passed in 2020 and his family didn't announce it for months. His catalog isn't something you license casually. It means something.

The visuals matched the energy. Messy desks, dark rooms, screens glowing. Not polished tech offices. Not sunny kitchens with marble countertops. This is where real work happens. Where things actually get made.

The voiceover doesn't promise to rescue you. It reframes the struggle.

> *"There's never been a better time to have a problem. To be stuck. To be out of ideas. To be out of your depth."*

By choosing DOOM, Anthropic says: we're going big and we want to make a statement about who we are and who our users can be. It doesn't feel like it's for everyone, yet somehow it's inspiring to all at the same time. This is for builders. For people who recognize the reference and understand what it means to put that track under your brand.

I said something out loud to my wife. I don't remember exactly what. Probably something like "wow… that's really great." She agreed.

From what I can tell, Mother London made the spot. Daniel Wolfe directed. The whole thing has swagger that Anthropic's usual voice doesn't have. Knowing their brand pretty well, this ad is an outlier. Their DNA is human-first, careful, considered. Not this. Not this much swagger.

But that's exactly what makes it work. The ad doesn't touch the usual Anthropic notes. It wants you to believe you can conquer things that haven't been done. That Dylan con I mentioned earlier? This ad leans all the way into it. And I'm here for it.

Credit to Anthropic for trusting their agency. For letting them swing. For approving a creative direction that doesn't look like the rest of their brand but absolutely captures something true about who uses their product.

The message isn't "we'll handle it for you." It's "we'll keep up with you."

Someone on X put it perfectly: "An AI commercial using MF DOOM's All Caps… god damnit they're trying to turn me."

That's the transfer working. Respect for DOOM becomes respect for the brand that understood why DOOM matters.

**The read:** Counterculture swagger. The ad for people who are building something and have taste about it.

## What the Leaderboard Reveals

The divide is pretty clean once you see it.

**Convenience ads** show small problems getting solved. Lost toys, dead fish, busy parents. The user is a little overwhelmed. Life is hard. Assistance is available. The AI is an appliance.

**Capability ads** show people in the middle of something meaningful. Training, creating, thinking, building. The user is already in motion and wants to go further. The AI is a collaborator.

Google and Meta are selling convenience.

ChatGPT and Claude are selling capability.

Grok is selling what you might be missing.

You can see it in the music choices alone. Google uses backing tracks I wouldn't remember five minutes later. Meta keeps things bright and kinetic. ChatGPT uses songs with real emotional texture, a little weight underneath the inspiration. Claude picks MF DOOM.

Here's the thing I keep coming back to: you can tell what a company thinks of you by how you feel about yourself after watching their ad.

The convenience ads make me feel like life is hard and I need help.

The capability ads make me feel like I might actually pull something off.

Dylan was probably right about advertising being a con. But some cons are worth falling for.

• • •

## Where I Landed

The ad didn't convert me. That's not how it played out.

I'd used all of these models exclusively for weeks, most for months at a time, trying to find the perfect one. ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Grok. Eventually I landed somewhere. Right now that's Claude. For reasons that deserve their own article.

So the ad wasn't conversion. But it was validation.

The ad made me feel like I was already in the right place. Like the company I was giving my money to actually understood something about who I am and what I'm trying to do. The MF DOOM choice wasn't just good marketing. It was a signal. It said: we get it. We know who our users are. We're not trying to be everything to everyone.

I'm exactly the target demographic. A maker who works late, respects craft, notices details most people skip. The ad was built for someone like me.

And it worked. Not to convert me. To make me a little more sure. A little more loyal. Maybe even a little bit of a fanboy.

Which, if you think about it, is kind of remarkable. Because I know how ads work. I know I'm being marketed to. I know that Claude and ChatGPT and Gemini are all trained on similar data and for most daily tasks the results are close enough that it probably doesn't matter which one I use.

And yet. Here I am. Reaching for Claude first. Writing an article about how much I liked their commercial.

This could change, by the way. These tools are evolving fast. My preferences might shift. Someone else might ship something that makes me switch. That's fine.

But right now? Claude has me. And the ad is part of the reason.

Isn't that what a good ad is supposed to do? Find its people?
